Pesto: The Green Sauce of Genoa

Pesto comes from Genoa, in Liguria, where basil grows sweet and tender in the mild coastal climate. The name comes from pestare, to crush, because the sauce was originally made by crushing basil, garlic, pine nuts, and cheese in a marble mortar. A food processor does the job today, but the principle is the same: crush, do not blend to a smooth paste, and never cook the sauce.

The ingredients

Real pesto Genovese contains fresh basil, pine nuts, garlic, Parmesan, pecorino, and extra virgin olive oil. The basil should be young and tender, with small leaves. Large, tough leaves can taste bitter. Use a mild olive oil, not a peppery one, because the oil should support the basil, not overpower it.

Pine nuts are expensive, but they are part of the classic recipe. Some cooks use walnuts instead, which is acceptable, but almonds and cashews change the flavour too much. The cheese is a mix of Parmesan and pecorino, which gives both sweetness and sharpness.

Making pesto

Put the basil, pine nuts, and garlic in a food processor and pulse until coarsely chopped. Add the grated cheeses and pulse again. Then, with the machine running, pour in the olive oil slowly until the sauce comes together. It should be coarse and textured, not smooth like a puree.

Taste and season with salt at the end, because the cheeses are already salty. If the pesto is too thick, add a little more oil. Do not add lemon juice or cream, which are not part of the recipe. A mortar and pestle gives the best texture, but a processor is fine if you pulse rather than blend.

Using pesto

Pesto should never be cooked. Toss it with hot pasta off the heat, adding a splash of the starchy pasta water to loosen the sauce and help it cling. The heat of the pasta warms the pesto without cooking the basil, so it stays bright green.

Trofie and fusilli are the classic shapes, because their twists trap the sauce. Pesto is also good on gnocchi, in a sandwich, or stirred into minestrone. Use about two tablespoons per person, and do not drown the pasta; pesto is a coating, not a soup.

Storing pesto

Fresh pesto keeps for about a week in the fridge in a sealed jar, with a thin layer of olive oil poured over the top to stop it darkening. It also freezes well in small portions, such as ice cube trays, for up to three months.

Pesto darkens when it meets air, so always press cling film onto the surface or cover it with oil. If the top goes dark, stir it; the pesto underneath is usually still bright green and fine to eat.

Pesto beyond pasta

Pesto is not only for pasta. Stir it into minestrone, spread it on a sandwich, or spoon it over grilled chicken or fish. It is good with boiled potatoes, with eggs, and with roasted vegetables. A spoonful of pesto turns a simple dish into something special.

There is also pesto rosso, a red pesto made with sun-dried tomatoes, almonds, and Parmesan. It is richer and sweeter than green pesto, and it is good with pasta, on bruschetta, or as a dip. Make a jar of each, and keep them in the fridge for a week, or freeze them in small portions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should pesto be cooked?

No. Pesto is never cooked. Toss it with hot pasta off the heat so the basil stays bright green and fresh.

What pasta goes best with pesto?

Trofie and fusilli are traditional, because their shapes trap the sauce. Linguine and gnocchi also work well.

Can I freeze pesto?

Yes. Freeze it in small portions, such as ice cube trays, for up to 3 months. Defrost in the fridge.
