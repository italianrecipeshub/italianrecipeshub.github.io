Lasagna: How to Build the Perfect Layers

Lasagna is a dish of patience. It is not hard to make, but it rewards the cook who takes time with the sauce, the bechamel, and the layering. A good lasagna slices cleanly, holds its layers, and tastes rich without being heavy. A bad one is a soggy pile. The difference is in the details, and they are all easy to learn.

The ragu

A lasagna ragu is a slow sauce of minced beef and pork, onion, carrot, celery, tomato, and a little wine. Brown the meat well first, because colour means flavour. Add the vegetables and cook them until soft, then add tomato and simmer gently for at least an hour. The sauce should be thick, not watery, because a thin sauce makes a soggy lasagna.

Season at the end, not the start, because the sauce reduces as it cooks. A good ragu can be made a day ahead, and it tastes better for it. Make more than you need and freeze the rest for a quick pasta dinner.

The bechamel

Bechamel is butter, flour, and milk, cooked into a smooth white sauce. Melt the butter, stir in an equal weight of flour, and cook for a minute. Add warm milk a little at a time, stirring constantly, until the sauce is smooth and coats a spoon. Season with salt, pepper, and a pinch of nutmeg.

The bechamel should be pourable, not stiff. If it is too thick, add a little more milk. If it is lumpy, whisk hard or pass it through a sieve. A good bechamel is what makes lasagna creamy and helps the layers stick together when sliced.

Pasta sheets: fresh, dried, or no-boil

Fresh pasta sheets give the best texture, but good dried sheets work well too. No-boil sheets are convenient, but they need enough sauce around them to soften, so be generous with ragu and bechamel. If you use dried sheets that need boiling, cook them briefly in salted water, then lay them on a clean tea towel so they do not stick together.

Whatever sheets you use, they should be covered in sauce on both sides. Dry edges turn hard and chewy in the oven. Trim sheets to fit the dish rather than folding them, because folds cook unevenly.

Layering and baking

Start with a thin layer of ragu on the bottom of the dish, then pasta, then bechamel, then ragu, then a little Parmesan. Repeat, usually three or four layers of pasta, and finish with pasta, bechamel, and a generous grating of Parmesan. The top layer should be mostly bechamel and cheese, which browns beautifully.

Bake at 180 degrees Celsius for 35 to 45 minutes, until bubbling and golden. Cover with foil for the first 20 minutes if the top browns too fast. Rest the lasagna for 10 to 15 minutes before serving, because this is what lets it slice cleanly. Serve with a simple green salad and nothing else.

Lasagna variations to try

Once you have the classic lasagna, try the variations. A vegetarian lasagna layers spinach and ricotta with tomato sauce and bechamel, and it is lighter than the meat version. A white lasagna uses mushrooms, chicken, and bechamel, with no tomato at all. A seafood lasagna, made with prawns and a light bechamel, is a special occasion dish.

The method stays the same whatever the filling: sauce, pasta, bechamel, and cheese, layered and baked until bubbling. Keep the layers even, rest the lasagna before slicing, and serve it with a simple salad. A variation is a new dish, not a new technique.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many layers should lasagna have?

Three or four layers of pasta is ideal. More layers make the lasagna tall and hard to serve; fewer make it more like baked pasta.

Can I make lasagna the day before?

Yes. Assemble it, cover and refrigerate, then bake the next day, adding 10 minutes to the baking time. It often tastes better the next day.

Do I need to boil no-boil lasagna sheets?

No. No-boil sheets soften in the sauce as they bake, but make sure they are fully covered in ragu and bechamel.
