Italian Sauces: Tomato, Pesto, Carbonara and More

Italian cooking is built on sauces, and most of them are simpler than they look. A great sauce needs few ingredients, good timing, and the confidence to stop adding things. Learn five of them well and you can feed a household for a month without repeating a meal.

Tomato sauce

A good tomato sauce starts with good tomatoes. Use whole peeled tomatoes, San Marzano if you can find them, crushed by hand. Cook garlic gently in olive oil until fragrant, add the tomatoes and a pinch of salt, and simmer for 20 to 30 minutes until it thickens slightly.

Keep it simple. Onion is optional, sugar is rarely needed if the tomatoes are good, and dried herbs are better than fresh ones cooked for a long time. Add fresh basil at the end. This sauce suits penne, rigatoni and spaghetti, and it freezes well in portions.

Pesto Genovese

Real pesto is made in a mortar or a food processor with fresh basil, pine nuts, garlic, Parmesan, pecorino and olive oil. The key is not to over-process it; the sauce should be coarse, not smooth. Add the oil slowly and season at the end.

Pesto should never be cooked. Toss it with hot pasta and a splash of pasta water off the heat, so the basil stays bright green. Store leftover pesto in a jar with a thin layer of oil on top to stop it darkening. Trofie and fusilli are the classic shapes for pesto.

Carbonara, without cream

Carbonara contains no cream. The sauce is eggs, Pecorino Romano, guanciale or pancetta, and black pepper. Cook the guanciale until crisp, then toss hot spaghetti with the crisped meat and its fat. Off the heat, add the beaten eggs and cheese, stirring quickly so the eggs thicken into a glossy sauce instead of scrambling.

The heat of the pasta cooks the eggs, so timing matters. Work fast, add a little pasta water if the sauce is too thick, and serve immediately. Use Pecorino Romano rather than Parmesan for the sharp, salty flavour that defines the dish.

Aglio e olio and ragu

Aglio e olio is garlic, olive oil, chilli and parsley, cooked gently and tossed with spaghetti. The trick is to cook the garlic slowly until golden, never brown, because burnt garlic turns bitter. A splash of pasta water binds the oil into a light sauce.

Ragu, often called Bolognese outside Italy, is a slow sauce of minced beef and pork, onion, carrot, celery, tomato and a little milk or wine. It needs at least an hour of gentle simmering, and two is better. Serve it with tagliatelle, not spaghetti, because the wide ribbons carry the meat.

Storing sauces

Tomato sauce and ragu freeze well for up to three months. Cool them quickly, portion into containers, and label with the date. Pesto keeps for a week in the fridge under oil, or frozen in ice cube trays for single portions.

Carbonara and aglio e olio do not store well and should be made fresh. They take ten minutes, so there is no reason to make them ahead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does carbonara have cream in it?

No. Real carbonara is eggs, Pecorino Romano, guanciale and black pepper. Cream is a common mistake outside Italy.

What pasta goes with ragu?

Tagliatelle or pappardelle. The wide, flat ribbons hold the meat sauce far better than spaghetti.

Can I freeze pesto?

Yes. Freeze it in small portions, such as ice cube trays, and defrost in the fridge. Add fresh oil if it looks dry.
