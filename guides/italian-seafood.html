Italian Seafood: Spaghetti alle Vongole, Branzino and More

Italy is surrounded by sea, and seafood is at the heart of its cooking, especially in the south and on the islands. Italian seafood cooking is light and fast: fish is grilled, fried, or tossed with pasta, and it is never hidden under heavy sauces. The rule is simple, buy the freshest fish you can, cook it briefly, and let it taste of the sea.

Spaghetti alle vongole

Spaghetti with clams is one of the great pasta dishes. Soak the clams in salted water for an hour so they release sand, then rinse them well. Cook garlic and chilli gently in olive oil, add the clams and a splash of white wine, and cover the pan until the clams open, about three minutes.

Toss the hot spaghetti into the pan with the clams, add a little pasta water, and finish with chopped parsley. Discard any clams that did not open. Do not add cheese to seafood pasta; it is not part of the tradition and it overpowers the delicate flavour.

Branzino al forno

Branzino, or European sea bass, is a classic whole fish for baking. Ask the fishmonger to gut and scale it. Score the skin, stuff the cavity with lemon slices, garlic, and herbs, and roast it at 200 degrees Celsius for about 20 minutes, until the flesh flakes easily.

Serve it whole, with olive oil and lemon, and let people help themselves. A whole fish looks impressive and stays moist, because the bones and skin protect the flesh. Fillets are quicker, but they dry out more easily, so watch them closely.

Fritto misto

Fritto misto is a mix of small fish and seafood, lightly floured and deep-fried until crisp. Use small fish such as whitebait, squid rings, and prawns. The oil should be hot, about 180 degrees Celsius, so the coating crisps quickly without absorbing oil.

Fry in small batches so the oil stays hot, drain on paper, and season with salt and lemon immediately. Serve fritto misto at once, with lemon wedges and nothing else. It is a starter or a main, and it is best eaten with the hands.

Buying and storing fish

Buy fish from a trusted fishmonger and use it the same day if you can. Fresh fish should smell of the sea, not of fish. The eyes should be clear, the gills red, and the flesh firm. If you buy fillets, they should look moist and translucent, not dry or dull.

Store fish in the fridge, covered, on ice if possible, and cook it within a day. Frozen fish is a good alternative, but defrost it slowly in the fridge, never in warm water or the microwave. And remember the Italian rule: never serve cheese with fish.

Planning a seafood feast

A seafood feast needs planning, because fish cooks quickly and waits for no one. Start with a simple antipasto, such as marinated anchovies or a seafood salad. Follow with a pasta course, such as spaghetti alle vongole, and then a main of grilled fish with salad. Finish with fruit, not a heavy dessert.

Plan on about 150 grams of fish per person for a main course, and buy the fish on the day. Cook the pasta first, then the fish, so everything is ready at once. Serve family style, with the fish on a platter and the sides in bowls, and let people help themselves. A seafood feast is a celebration, and it should feel relaxed, not formal.

Frequently Asked Questions

What fish is best for spaghetti alle vongole?

Small clams, such as vongole veraci. Soak them in salted water first to release sand, and discard any that do not open.

Should I put cheese on seafood pasta?

No. In Italian cooking, cheese is not served with fish or seafood, because it overpowers the delicate flavour.

How do I know if fish is fresh?

It should smell of the sea, not fishy. Eyes should be clear, gills red, and flesh firm and springy.
