Italian Cooking Techniques: Soffritto, Mantecatura and More

Italian cooking looks simple because the techniques are few and they are done well. There are no complicated sauces, no fussy presentations, and no long lists of ingredients. Instead there are a handful of methods, learned properly and repeated until they are instinctive. Master these five techniques and you can cook most Italian food.

Soffritto

Soffritto is the base of most Italian cooking: onion, carrot, and celery, finely chopped and cooked gently in olive oil until soft and sweet. It takes ten minutes, and it cannot be rushed. The vegetables should soften without browning, because browning changes the flavour.

Soffritto appears in soups, sauces, and braises. It is the foundation that everything else is built on. Learn to chop finely and evenly, cook slowly, and season lightly, and your sauces will taste better immediately.

Tostatura

Tostatura is toasting the rice in a risotto, or the pasta in some dishes, before adding liquid. The grains are stirred in hot fat for one to two minutes until they are hot and slightly translucent. This helps them stay separate and absorb liquid evenly.

Tostatura is also used for nuts and breadcrumbs. Toasted pine nuts, almonds, and breadcrumbs add crunch and flavour to pasta, salads, and vegetables. Toast them in a dry pan over medium heat, shaking often, and take them off the heat before they look done, because they keep cooking.

Mantecatura

Mantecatura is the final step in risotto and pasta, where cold butter and grated cheese are beaten into the hot dish off the heat. It gives risotto its shine and creaminess, and it binds a pasta sauce to the pasta.

The key is to work off the heat, with cold butter, and to stir vigorously. The sauce should be glossy and clinging, not oily or separated. Mantecatura is the difference between a good risotto and a great one, and it takes one minute.

Salting, tasting and finishing

Salt is the most important ingredient in Italian cooking, and it is used at every stage. Salt the pasta water like the sea, salt the soffritto lightly, and taste the sauce before serving. Taste as you cook, not just at the end, because seasoning builds in layers.

Finish a dish with good olive oil, fresh herbs, and a little grated cheese. A drizzle of oil over a finished soup or pasta adds flavour and shine. Fresh basil should be torn, not chopped, and added at the end so it stays bright. These small finishes are what make a dish taste finished rather than merely cooked.

Knife skills and preparation

Good Italian cooking starts before the stove is on. Chop the onion, carrot, and celery for the soffritto finely and evenly, so they cook at the same rate. Crush garlic with the flat of a knife rather than chopping it, if you want a gentler flavour. Tear basil by hand, because a knife bruises the leaves and darkens them.

Prepare everything before you start cooking, a habit chefs call mise en place. It means you are not chopping while the garlic burns, and you can focus on the pan. Keep a bowl for scraps beside the board, wipe the board between tasks, and taste as you cook. These small habits make the difference between a stressed cook and a calm one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is soffritto?

A base of finely chopped onion, carrot and celery cooked gently in olive oil until soft. It is the foundation of soups, sauces and braises.

What is mantecatura?

The final step in risotto and pasta, where cold butter and cheese are beaten in off the heat to make the dish glossy and creamy.

Why should pasta water be salty?

It is the only chance to season the pasta itself. The water should taste like the sea, about a teaspoon of salt per litre.
